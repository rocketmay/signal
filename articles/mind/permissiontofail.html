Permission to fail

May Chan // June 5, 2026

Success is a result of many failures, not many successes.

For high level performance training, many athletes approach their training with the mindset that they must always be performing or striving to perform at their best. The success of a practice session depends on how well they feel they performed.

At the most basic level, if an athlete scored high in practice then that was a good practice, and if they had a terrible score, it was a bad practice. The evaluation is usually accompanied by some negative review of their condition on the day to explain why their performance was poor – I’m tired, I ate too much before training, I’m sore from other activities, my gun isn’t set up right.

This approach to self-evaluation is a mistake.

Training is not about performance, it’s about improvement. And improvement does not happen by performing well. Performing well is a result of improvement.

So evaluating your training based on your performance is fundamentally backward.

The correct way to evaluate your training is to look at what you were trying to accomplish in your training session, and determine how well you were able to work on that specific focus.

Fundamental to training this way, and one of the most critical mindset shifts you can make in your training is giving yourself permission to fail.

You need to give yourself permission to shoot a bad score, permission to make a bad shot. You need to be allowed, by yourself, to make mistakes.

Mistakes and bad shots are not negative, they’re part of the learning process. When they happen during training, your mindset should be framed around whether or not that mistake was related to your training focus, not the ‘poor’ result.

For example, if you’re working on pulling the trigger smoothly, but your arm dropped as you pulled the trigger and you scored poorly– What’s going through your mind?

“Ugh, what a terrible shot. I always make this mistake. I can’t get a good score anymore.”

Or:

“That was a mistake. I scored really low. That’s okay, because I’m allowed to make bad shots. But my trigger pull was smooth, the result was because of something other than my trigger. So actually, this shot was a good shot related to what I’m training.”

The first option is natural and perhaps cathartic in a way, but it doesn’t help you improve. The second option keeps you focused on your training and what you’re trying to work on. And because you gave yourself permission to fail, you can easily move onto the next shot.

When you can forgive your mistakes, you can focus your attention on improvement. Your inner dialogue becomes streamlined and focused on your training goals for the day:

“That was another mistake, but I felt like my trigger pull was fine.”

“That shot felt good, and my trigger pull was good.”

“That shot scored a 10, but my trigger was terrible. It was definitely a lucky shot.”

“That shot my trigger pull was poor. I was distracted trying to get a high score.”

“That shot my trigger pull was poor, but I’m working on it, so it’s okay.”

Conclusion

Sometimes, your goal for the day is to score your best. You might be doing a practice match, you might be setting a benchmark, or you might just want to shoot your best for fun. In those scenarios, mistakes are costly because they directly impact the result of your session.

But for the majority of your training, you should explicitly give yourself room to make mistakes, understanding that it’s part of learning. This will reduce a lot of pressure on your mind and free up your attention to focus on the important elements of your training.
