Building effective habits for top performance

May Chan // June 8, 2026

Let’s talk about the meta level of performance, that is, not individual acts of performance but the layer above that. The framework of performance, that influences the quality of your performances.

On any given day, you step up to the line and you start shooting, yes? Have you ever thought about what exactly determines if you will have a good or bad performance that day? Have you ever thought about how can you be sure that when you need to have a good performance, you will have a good performance?

Managing this meta level is a skill that is extremely important for competitive athletes. After all, you need to be able to perform at your best when you are at a competition. You can’t just postpone the match because you aren’t feeling it that day.

You might think that this is simply a matter of chance or luck, but hopefully not because you’d be wrong.

Achieving consistent performances that hit the top of your current abilities is a skill that anyone can develop.

Let’s get into it.

Take ownership of your performance

This isn’t so much a tip as it is an imperative. Make a decision to own your performance. Something as important as your ability to perform your best shouldn’t be left to chance or luck, right?

Your mind is an incredibly powerful tool, and how you use it matters. If you believe you have no control over your performance, your mind will make that a reality. If you believe that you do have control, then, incredibly, your mind will make that a reality instead.

You’re in control. Your mind and body have the power to perform at your best, every single day. You might not believe it just yet, you might not be there today, but you need to accept that this power exists and that you are already using it, for better or worse.

Got it?

Let’s give you some tools.

Repetition builds habit, habit builds consistency

You’ve heard the phrase ‘Practice makes Perfect’? Or perhaps the alternate version ‘Practice makes Permanent’? This is a more nuanced and broader exploration of what that means, particularly in the mental realm.

Performance, and in particular consistent performance, is about being so comfortable with executing a skill (or a set of skills) that you can do it effortlessly. You’ve practiced so much that it’s been baked into your subconscious and you can perform on autopilot.

And when we’re talking about meta performance, we’re talking about the mental side of the sport. When we execute a specific mental routine so often that it becomes baked into our subconscious, we call this a habit.

When you do dry fire practice day after day, you’re repeating the action of pulling the trigger until it becomes effortless. When you tell yourself, ‘I’m going to perform my best today’, every day before you practice, you’re repeating this mental process until it becomes a habit.

When you’ve ingrained this habit so deeply into your subconscious that it becomes second nature, you'll be able to perform at your best no matter what happens on any given day.

Negative repetition builds negative performance

Your mind is powerful, so when you repeat negativity, you are actively lowering your ability to perform well. I have to say this explicitly because many people have the habit of negativity. Take a moment to think critically about how you evaluate your performance, how you evaluate your moods and your skills.

Do any of these sound familiar?

  • I always shoot poorly at the end of practice
  • I can’t focus today, work (or school) was too tiring
  • I was too hungry, it distracted me so much I couldn’t shoot well
  • People kept bothering me and I couldn’t concentrate
  • I suck

Everyone has negative thoughts. It’s only human. I’m definitely not telling you to lie to yourself, or to not acknowledge when you’re feeling or performing poorly.

But the question is, do you have a habit of negativity?

When you make a mistake, do you get frustrated at yourself? That’s a habit.

When you are doing extremely well, do you get more and more anxious about ‘losing’ your flow and shooting a bad shot? That’s a habit.

Negativity can be comforting. It feels bad, but it also gives you a reason for disappointing performances. It relieves the pressure to perform and do well, because you are proactively taking the steps to embrace your failure.

I did badly today, here’s why.

And that’s… fine. If you want to feel comforted about your bad performance, by all means, be negative. But if you want to be the best that you can be, if you want to push yourself to consistent, repeatable excellence, you cannot afford to have a habit of negativity.

Positive repetition builds positive performance

The mind is a powerful thing. Incredibly powerful, so much so that as much as you can build negative habits that let you land softly when you inevitably fail, you can also build positive habits that will inevitably lift you to greatness.

In the examples above, let’s rewrite our negative thoughts into positive thoughts that can be used to build positive habits.

  • I can shoot well at all stages of practice
  • Work (or school) was tiring, but I’m still going to focus as best as I can.
  • I’m hungry, but it doesn’t stop me from shooting well.
  • People keep bothering me, this is good practice for distractions during competition.
  • I’m doing my best.

“Can” is a powerful word here. When you “can” do something, you might not be able to do it every time, but you acknowledge that it’s possible or even probable.

Reframing your distractions and troubles as challenges to overcome puts you in a mindset of power and control. Yes, you’re stressed, but you’re going to put your best effort in.

With practice (and building this positive habit), you’ll have confidence that you’ll be able to perform perfectly regardless of, or even in spite of, any and all obstacles that have been put in your path.

Again, this isn’t toxic positivity. This is giving yourself permission to perform your best. This is practicing putting your mind into a state where it can perform its best. If you practice positive habits, they will become second nature, ingrained so deeply in your subconsciousness that you couldn’t perform poorly even if you wanted to.

New habits overwrite old habits

If you have negative habits that you wish to get rid of, the most effective way is to replace them with new, positive habits.

If you have a habit of always starting slowly and taking forever to warm up that you want to fix, you simply need to build a habit of always starting in perfect condition.

Notice that I did not say, “Continue to notice and criticize yourself when you start slowly.” And yet, it’s so tempting when you’re trying to get rid of a bad habit, to continue to reinforce that bad habit by noticing that you’re still doing the bad habit, and feeling bad about it. Negative repetition builds negative performance.

So, the next time you start practice or start a match, you tell yourself, “I always start in perfect condition.” Or if that’s too much for you, “I can start in perfect condition, and today I will.” If, that day, you have a terrible start, don’t worry about it, let it slide. And if you have a great start, nod to yourself and repeat, ‘I always start in perfect condition, after all.’

Then, in the next practice (and the one after that, and the one after that…), tell yourself the same thing. Think of it like a mantra that you repeat. Do it until it becomes a habit, until you have no choice but to start in perfect condition every single time.

Notice how at no point do you ever go back to your old habit. You don’t need to notice bad starts anymore, because they’re not important to you. You don’t need to reinforce those negative thoughts. If you have a bad start one day, okay it happens, move on. Negative thoughts and habits don’t help you perform at your best, so why would you keep them?

Winners know they’ll win

I’ll leave this final thought with you. What makes a champion, a champion?

Champions win because they know they’ll win. Not because they rely on past success, but because they have built a habit of always performing at their absolute best. Champions have a habit of winning.

When you think about the highest echelons of sport, every single contender to the podium has a champion mindset. They have the skills and physique to win, of course. But they have the mental habits to perform at the top of their level, every time.

At the highest level, people who don’t have a champion mindset cannot be champions. How could someone who doesn’t believe they can win, take victory against someone who’s embedded victory into the deepest part of their subconscious?

Do you want to be a champion, too? To be the best you can be? With what you’ve learned in this article, you now know exactly how to get there.
