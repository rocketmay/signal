Shot Timing Development (500+)

May Chan // October 12, 2026

This article should be quite helpful for athletes shooting at around the 500 point level.

One critical aspect of skill development is the timing of your shot process. A natural tendency of athletes in the beginner and intermediate stages is to try to increase accuracy by focusing on pulling the trigger when the sights are perfectly aimed at the target. It makes sense, right? Why take the shot if your sights aren’t aligned?

However, in trying to shoot only when the sights are aligned, this introduces hesitation and tension that interrupts the shot’s natural flow. This leads to flinches and jerks, which have a big impact on the score.

So then, how does one achieve good aim while also avoiding introducing hesitation or tension? Or, to put it in a more positive phrasing, what can you do to be able to pull the trigger smoothly when the sights are aligned?

Approach the problem from the other direction

To put it simply, pulling the trigger when the sights are aligned is backwards. The perfect shot execution aligns the sights when the trigger is pulled. The trigger action is the determinant of the timing, not the process of aiming.

The reason for this is that at the highest level of execution, the smoothness and precision with which the trigger is pulled becomes the most important factor for shot accuracy. All imperfections, extraneous forces, hesitation and impulses must be reduced to a minimum to obtain a perfectly smooth trigger pull.

The first step toward achieving this comes from placing the emphasis on the trigger pull ahead of aiming the sights, so that the trigger finger becomes the main driving force of each shot.

The following drill is designed to develop this skill.

The Drill: Timed execution

Before I get into the drill, let me establish two important points:

First, this drill is best done if you cannot see the result of the shot. If you are using paper targets, you can flip the paper around so that you are firing at a blank target. If you are using electronic targets, you can cover the screen while shooting.

Second, and somewhat related to the first, it is extremely tempting to want to evaluate the drill based on where the pellet hits the target, but this will just distract you from the exercise. Do your best to avoid this temptation. Throughout the drill, keep in mind that the score does not matter.

Rather than score or shot placement, the success metric for this drill is the timing of the trigger pull. Your goal is to pull the trigger at a certain amount of time after you align the sights. The timing will depend on your current shot process, but a good place to start is around four seconds.

Every shot you take for the duration of the drill (ideally an entire sessions' worth of shots), you must activate the trigger after the decided timing, in this case four seconds. So, after you raise the gun from the bench and align the sights (typically, above the target, after which you slowly descend to the final position), you start counting.

On the count of four, you pull the trigger.

Remember, it doesn't matter where the shot lands, only that you successfully pull the trigger at the count of four.

The method of pulling the trigger is very specific. From the moment you start counting, until the moment the gun fires, you are focusing on pulling the trigger as smoothly and evenly as possible.

Your inner monologue could be as follows: "Raise the gun"… "Align the sights"… "Slowly lower the sights" (to the final position while counting)... "One" (Start squeezing)… "Two"… "Three" (Steadily increase pressure)… "Four" (Shot goes off)”.

At first, it might take all your concentration to steadily pull the trigger such that it fires at the count of four. Your shots will definitely be all over the place (but you're not worrying about that, remember?)

As you practice this drill, you will become more and more comfortable with the timing. The more comfortable the timing becomes, the more you will have the mental space to concentrate on other aspects of your shot process. This typically manifests itself as a feeling of triviality or even boredom.

As you get more comfortable with this process, you should find that your shots are a lot more consistent. You will flinch less, force shots less often, and feel like the entire process becomes a lot more smoother and repeatable.

Conclusion

At its core, this drill is designed to replace bad habits with good ones. Forcing shots, hesitating too long, trying to wait for the perfect moment before pulling the trigger, are all bad habits that will impact your accuracy.

This drill starts building out the muscle memory that will let you execute every shot in a consistent, repeatable manner.
